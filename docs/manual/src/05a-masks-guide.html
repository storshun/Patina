Masks and smart materials: a beginner's guide

If you have never used Substance Painter or a similar tool, masks and smart materials can be the least obvious parts of Patina. They are also what gets you from "a model painted one color" to "a believable, worn object" in a few minutes. This page explains what they are and why they work the way they do, then walks through a complete example from an empty model to a smart material you can reuse on any other model.

How to read this pageThe first half explains the ideas, and the second half (the walkthrough) has you build one material. If you prefer learning by doing, open samples/Suzanne.glb and jump straight to the walkthrough. The reference pages Layers and masks and Folders, solo and smart materials list every setting.

The big idea: layers of material, shaped by masks

In a 2D paint program you usually paint color directly onto the picture. Texture painters work differently. A real worn object is made of several materials on top of each other: steel, a coat of paint over it, dirt on the paint, a rust stain here and there. Patina builds a surface the same way, as a stack of layers, each holding one material:

  • The bottom layer covers everything, for example blue paint.
  • The next layer up holds a different material, for example bare steel, but it should only show where the paint has worn off.
  • Above that, dirt, but only in the creases.

The part in italics is the mask. A mask is a black-and-white image, wrapped around the model like the textures are, that decides where a layer shows:

That is all a mask is. The rest of this page is about the different ways of making that black-and-white image: painting it by hand, letting Patina work it out from the shape of the model, or combining both.

Why not just paint the rust on directly?

You can (a paint layer and a rust material will do it). Masks are better for most surface work for three reasons:

  • Nothing is destroyed. The paint layer is still complete underneath the rust. Erase some of the mask and the paint comes back exactly as it was. Painted directly, the paint under the rust would be gone.
  • You can change your mind about the material later. Swap the rust layer's material for dried mud and the same shape now holds mud. With direct painting you would repaint everything.
  • Masks can be calculated. Patina can make a mask from the model's shape: every edge, every crease, every upward-facing surface. That is a lot of careful painting you don't have to do, and it works on any model, which is what makes smart materials possible.

Fill layers: the layer type masks are made for

Most of the layers in a material stack are fill layers ([[icon:add_fill]] Fill layer in the Layers panel). A fill layer covers the whole model with one material, and then its mask cuts it down. Two consequences that confuse people at first:

  • When you add a fill layer, the whole model changes, because a new fill layer has a white mask: it shows everywhere.
  • When you paint on a fill layer, you are painting its mask, not color. Brush strokes paint white and reveal the material, and the eraser paints black and hides it. The material comes from the layer settings, not the brush.

So there are two ways to start a fill layer, depending on what you want:

Paint layers and folders can have masks too ([[icon:mask]] Add mask on the layer's row), and adjustment layers use their mask to limit where the adjustment applies. Everything on this page applies to them as well.

The kinds of mask

Patina has three ways of making a mask, and you will usually mix them.

Painted masks

Click the mask thumbnail on the layer's row (or the Mask button) and paint. While you paint a mask, the viewport label says [mask]. The Paint tool paints white (or the gray set by Mask value in the Properties panel), the Erase tool (E) paints black, and all the usual brush settings apply (size, hardness, flow, pressure, symmetry). To go back to painting the layer itself, click the layer's name. A painted mask follows exactly what you did, so it suits details that belong to this model only: a scorch mark next to an exhaust, a worn patch where a hand grips a handle.

You can also make a mask from a selection: select faces, an island or a material slot, then right-click the layer ▸ Mask from the face selection.

Smart masks

A smart mask is worked out from the shape of the model, using maps that Patina bakes (calculates once and stores) for it: curvature, ambient occlusion and thickness. Pick one in the layer settings, in the Smart mask combo. Patina bakes whatever it needs the first time.

Every smart mask has the same four controls, and they are worth understanding, because tuning them is most of the work:

  • Level is the threshold: how much of the model passes. For Edges, a higher level keeps only the sharpest edges and a lower level spreads the wear further onto the flat areas.
  • Contrast is how sharp the boundary is. Low contrast gives a soft, gradual fade, and high contrast a crisp line, like paint chipped off.
  • Noise and Noise scale break up the boundary with organic noise. A smart mask with no noise looks machine-made (every edge worn by exactly the same amount), and a little noise is usually what makes it believable.
  • Invert swaps black and white: Edges inverted is "everywhere except the edges".

Mask effect stacks: combining masks

Sometimes one smart mask is not enough: wear on the edges, but only on the top half, softened, with a few spots scraped away by hand. A mask effect stack builds a mask from several steps: smart masks, painted masks, noise, levels, blur and invert, each with a blend mode such as multiply or add. Choose Add a mask effect… in the mask settings to turn a plain mask into a stack. You don't need stacks to get started; come back to them when a single smart mask can't do what you want.

Smart and painted together

A layer can have a smart mask and a painted mask at the same time. They multiply: the layer shows where both are white. That is the usual way to fix a smart mask that is right almost everywhere: keep the smart mask, then paint black on the mask where the wear shouldn't be (the inside of a handle, say).

Seeing a mask

A mask is hard to judge from the finished material, so Patina can show you the mask itself:

  • The Layer Mask view. Choose Layer Mask in the view combo of the viewport bar. The model shows the selected layer's mask in black and white: the effective mask, meaning the painted mask, the smart mask and any effect stack combined, exactly as the layer uses it. Tune Level and Contrast while you watch it, then switch back to Material.
  • Solo. Alt+click a layer's eye to show only that layer (over the base), so you can see what it adds. Alt+click again to show everything.
  • Toggling. Click a layer's eye to hide it and compare. The compare view shows before and after side by side.

Walkthrough: a worn metal finish, step by step

This builds a blue painted metal that is worn through on the edges, dirty in the creases and rusty in one spot, on Suzanne, and then turns it into a smart material. It takes about ten minutes. Open samples/Suzanne.glb (File › Open…).

  1. Make a folder for the material. Click [[icon:add_folder]] Folder and double-click its name to rename it, for example Worn blue metal. Everything else goes inside it, which keeps the stack tidy and lets you save the whole folder as a smart material at the end. New layers go into the selected folder.
  2. The base: blue paint. With the folder selected, pick Car Paint Blue in the Materials panel and click [[icon:add_fill]] Fill layer. The whole model turns blue. Rename the layer Paint. This layer needs no mask: it is the bottom of the material.
  3. Bare steel on the edges. Pick Steel Scratched and click [[icon:add_fill]] Fill layer again. The model is now all steel, because the new layer's mask is white. In the layer settings, set Smart mask to Edges. The steel retreats to the edges and corners, and the paint comes back everywhere else. Rename it Bare steel.
  4. Tune the edge mask. Switch the view to Layer Mask. Move Level up until only the edges you want stay white (around 0.65 here), and set Contrast to taste. It now looks too clean, so add Noise (about 0.5) and try a few Noise scale values: the wear breaks up into irregular patches. Switch back to Material to see the result.
  5. Dirt in the creases. Pick Mud, [[icon:add_fill]] Fill layer, set the smart mask to Occlusion, lower the Level until the dirt sits only in the deep corners (around 0.2), add a little noise, and lower the layer's Opacity to about 85% so the paint shows through faintly.
  6. A hand-painted rust patch. Pick Rust Heavy, then the arrow next to [[icon:add_fill]] Fill layer ▸ Fill layer with a black mask. Nothing changes, because the mask is black. Click the layer's mask thumbnail, choose a soft brush and paint where you want rust: the strokes paint white into the mask and reveal it. The Erase tool (E) takes it back.
"I painted but nothing changed"You are painting the mask of a fill layer whose mask is already white. Painting white on white does nothing. Erase instead (E), or start the layer with a black mask.
"My brush paints the wrong thing"Check the viewport label: [mask] means strokes go into the selected layer's mask. Click the layer's name to paint the layer instead, or select a different layer. On a fill layer there is nothing to paint but the mask.

Smart materials: saving the recipe

Look at what the folder you just built contains: three fill layers with smart masks, plus one painted mask. Apart from the rust patch, nothing in it is specific to Suzanne. "Blue paint, steel on the edges with Level 0.65 and some noise, dirt in the occluded areas at 85%" is a recipe, and the masks are recalculated from whatever model it is used on. A smart material is exactly that: a saved folder (or layer) of settings that you can apply to any model.

Save it

  1. Remove or keep the hand-painted parts. The rust patch was painted for Suzanne, in Suzanne's UV layout. On another model it would land in a random spot, so leave it out of a general-purpose smart material: delete the layer, or move it out of the folder.
  2. Right-click the folder ▸ Save as smart material… and give it a name. Leave Include painted textures and masks off, so only the settings are stored.

Use it

Open another model, click [[icon:smart]] Smart materials above the layer list and pick your material. It arrives as one folder, and Patina bakes the maps its smart masks need. The edges, cavities and occlusion are those of the new model:

A smart material is a starting point, not a finished result. Every model is different, so open the folder and adjust: raise an Edges level on a model with many sharp edges, scale materials up on a large object, paint a few corrections into a mask.

Built-in smart materials

Patina comes with nineteen smart materials ([[icon:smart]] Smart materials), listed in Folders, solo and smart materials: worn painted metal, scratched painted steel, rusted iron, aged brass, oxidized copper, heat-tinted metal, worn gold trim, worn sci-fi panels, dusty and grimy plastic, worn leather, dirty fabric, weathered and mossy wood, mossy stone, living skin, and three overlays that go on top of another material: snow cover, edge wear + grime, and stylized edge highlights for toon and hand-painted materials. Open their folders to see how they are built: each one is a few fill layers with smart masks, like the walkthrough. Scratched painted steel also shows a mask effect stack (paint worn away by an edge mask and by chips from a noise mask), Heat-tinted metal stacks three height gradients into color bands, and Living skin uses the Thin parts mask to warm the ears and nose.

Your own smart materials are files in ~/.local/share/patina/user/smart_materials (on Windows, %LOCALAPPDATA%\patina\user\smart_materials). Copy one to share it with someone else.

Tips for good results

  • Think in physical order. Stack layers the way the object was made and aged: base metal, primer, paint, then wear, dirt and stains on top. Wear that reveals metal is easiest as a steel layer above the paint with an edge mask.
  • Fewer, better layers. Three or four well-tuned fill layers usually look better than ten.
  • Always add some noise. Real wear and dirt are never uniform.
  • Check the mask, not just the result. The Layer Mask view shows you immediately whether a mask catches the right places.
  • Bake again after big mesh or UV changes. Smart masks come from baked maps. If you change the model or its UVs a lot, use Bake › All mesh maps so the masks follow.
  • Low-poly models work. The edge mask is measured on the surface at a fixed scale, so a simple box gets clean worn edges, and a smooth sphere is not treated as one big edge.

Glossary

TermMeaning
LayerOne step in the stack: paint, a fill (one material everywhere), an adjustment, or a folder of other layers.
MaskA black-and-white image deciding where a layer shows. White shows, black hides, gray blends.
Fill layerA layer that covers the model with one material. Painting on it paints its mask.
Smart mask (generator)A mask calculated from the model's shape: edges, cavities, occlusion, thin parts, direction, height, noise or material slot.
Mask effect stackA mask built from several steps (smart masks, paint, noise, levels, blur) combined with blend modes.
Bake / mesh mapsMaps Patina calculates once from the model (curvature, ambient occlusion, thickness, position) that smart masks are built from.
CurvatureHow convex (edges) or concave (creases) each point of the surface is.
Ambient occlusion (AO)How much of the surrounding light reaches each point. Low in corners and crevices.
Smart materialA saved folder or layer: materials plus smart masks, reusable on any model.
SoloShowing one layer on its own (Alt+click its eye).
Non-destructiveNothing is painted over permanently: every layer and mask can still be changed or removed.
